# Evidence Bundle: Food and Consumer Product Safety

## Thesis

Consumer health products — supplements, personal care items, packaged foods, and household wellness products — vary dramatically in safety, quality, and actual efficacy. The combination of minimal pre-market FDA regulation for supplements, opaque ingredient disclosure practices, sophisticated marketing language, and asymmetric consumer information creates a landscape where purchasing decisions made without evidence-based ingredient analysis carry meaningful health risk. Evidence-based ingredient analysis prevents harm, separates genuine quality signals from marketing theater, and helps consumers allocate spending toward products with demonstrated safety and appropriate evidence for their intended use. The default should be informed scrutiny, not trust.

## Primary Authors and Frameworks

- Environmental Working Group (EWG) — Skin Deep database, Food Scores database. Consumer-accessible ingredient risk summaries.
- ConsumerLab — Independent supplement testing, label accuracy, and contaminant screening.
- NSF International — Third-party supplement certification (NSF Certified for Sport, NSF Content Claim Standard).
- USP (United States Pharmacopeia) — Dietary supplement verification program.
- Informed Sport / Informed Choice — UK-based third-party testing for banned substance contamination.
- FDA — Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act (DSHEA, 1994), GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) list, warning letters.
- EFSA (European Food Safety Authority) — Often applies a more precautionary standard than FDA. EU restriction lists are a useful secondary reference.

## Key Lenses

**Third-party certification** — The most important single quality signal for supplements. NSF Certified for Sport, USP Verified, Informed Sport, and ConsumerLab Approved products have been independently tested for label accuracy (correct ingredient identity and dose), contaminant absence (heavy metals, microbial contamination), and in some cases banned substance absence (critical for competitive athletes). The absence of certification does not mean a product is unsafe, but it means the quality claim is unverified.

**Proprietary blends** — A regulatory artifact that allows supplement manufacturers to list combined ingredient weights without disclosing individual doses. This makes it impossible to assess whether any individual ingredient is present at a clinically relevant dose or to evaluate safety at actual intake levels. Flag proprietary blends as a transparency concern.

**Heavy metal contamination** — Protein powders (particularly plant-based), herbal supplements, and products with inorganic mineral forms can contain significant heavy metal loads (lead, arsenic, cadmium, mercury). Consumer Lab and Clean Label Project testing has repeatedly found heavy metal contamination in popular products, sometimes at levels exceeding EPA drinking water standards per serving. This is an ingredient-independent manufacturing quality concern.

**Endocrine-disrupting ingredients** — Several ingredient categories have documented endocrine-disrupting activity at physiologically relevant exposure levels. Key examples: phthalates (plasticizers in flexible packaging, personal care products), parabens (preservatives with weak estrogenic activity), triclosan (antibacterial, now restricted in the US but present in some products), certain UV filters (oxybenzone, benzophenone). Risk is dose and exposure context dependent — cumulative daily-use products matter more than occasional exposure.

**Artificial dyes** — FD&C dyes (Red 40, Yellow 5, Yellow 6, Blue 1, Blue 2) are approved in the US but carry mandatory warning labels in the EU for potential behavioral effects in children. Evidence in adults is limited, but for products consumed daily, cleaner alternatives exist without meaningful cost or efficacy trade-off.

**Ingredient bioavailability and form** — The chemical form of a nutrient determines how well it is absorbed and utilized. Examples of form-quality differentials: magnesium oxide (poor absorption, low bioavailability) vs. magnesium glycinate or threonate (high bioavailability); cyanocobalamin (synthetic B12, requires conversion) vs. methylcobalamin (bioactive form); calcium carbonate (requires stomach acid for absorption) vs. calcium citrate (absorbed without stomach acid, important for people on PPIs). A supplement with the wrong form provides little benefit at any dose.

**Marketing language flags** — Specific terms indicate marketing rather than evidence: "proprietary blend," "clinically studied formula" (product may have been in a single small trial), "quantum," "synergistic," "mega-dose," "doctor formulated" (no regulatory meaning), "all natural" (hemlock is all natural). Conversely, legitimate quality signals include: certification logos from recognized third parties, disclosure of full ingredient list with individual doses, NSF or ISO manufacturing certifications, and reference to specific peer-reviewed studies.

## Evidence Standards

This bundle favors:
- Third-party independent testing results (ConsumerLab, NSF, USP testing reports)
- Regulatory toxicology assessments from FDA, EFSA, and ECHA
- Pharmacokinetic studies establishing bioavailability of specific ingredient forms
- Epidemiological studies on ingredient exposure and health outcomes with adequate power

This bundle treats with caution:
- Industry-sponsored ingredient safety studies
- Single case reports as evidence of population-level risk
- EWG risk scores without cross-referencing primary regulatory sources (EWG can be more precautionary than regulatory science supports)
- Efficacy claims based on in vitro studies without human pharmacokinetic evidence

## Known Limits

**Regulatory landscape is jurisdiction-specific.** An ingredient banned in the EU may be legal in the US; a product with GRAS status has passed a different review threshold than European Novel Food authorization. Context matters, and regulatory approval is not equivalence across jurisdictions.

**Cumulative exposure is the real risk picture.** Individual product ingredient levels often fall below single-study risk thresholds. The actual concern is daily cumulative exposure across multiple products (personal care, food, water, supplements). This is difficult to precisely quantify but should be acknowledged as the real framework for risk assessment.

**The evidence base for long-term ingredient safety is incomplete.** Many ingredients in regular commercial use have limited long-term human safety data. Absence of evidence of harm is not evidence of absence of harm. Conservative choices for daily-use products are reasonable even when definitive harm data is absent.

**Product composition changes without notice.** Formulations change; a certification valid at purchase may not reflect the current product. Certifications should be current (check expiration dates on certification logos) rather than historical.
