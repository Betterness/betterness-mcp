# Evidence Bundle: Women's Health

## Thesis

Women's health requires cycle-phase-aware, hormone-informed approaches that standard clinical guidelines — developed predominantly from male-subject research — systematically fail to provide. The menstrual cycle is not a complication of health science; it is a biological signal system with diagnostic utility. Hormonal fluctuations across the cycle affect exercise capacity, recovery rate, metabolic function, sleep architecture, nutritional requirements, pain sensitivity, and injury risk in ways that are clinically meaningful and largely ignored in standard practice. Similarly, the perimenopause transition — often beginning years before the final menstrual period — produces physiological changes that require distinct management frameworks. Women are not small men, and the health science built primarily on male-subject studies fails them when applied without adaptation.

## Primary Authors and Frameworks

- Stacy Sims, PhD — Roar: How to Match Your Food and Fitness to Your Unique Female Physiology. Female athlete physiology, cycle-based training.
- RED-S consensus statement (IOC, 2014, updated 2018) — Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport.
- Female Athlete Science and Translational Research (FASTR) programs — Exercise physiology in female athletes.
- Menstrual cycle periodization research — Phase-specific training adaptation studies.
- Perimenopause and menopause literature — MENQOL, WHI follow-up data, hormone therapy risk-benefit reassessments.

## Key Lenses

**Menstrual cycle phases** — The cycle provides a repeating hormonal context that modulates physiology meaningfully:
- **Follicular phase (day 1–13):** Rising estrogen. Lower resting temperature. Better glycogen utilization. Higher pain tolerance. Improved response to high-intensity training.
- **Ovulatory phase (~day 14):** Peak estrogen and LH surge. Peak strength and power. Elevated ligament laxity (ACL injury risk).
- **Luteal phase (day 15–28):** Rising progesterone. Elevated core temperature (0.3–0.5°C). Higher resting HR. Increased fat oxidation. Reduced heat tolerance. Greater carbohydrate need around sessions.
- **Menstrual phase (day 1–5):** Prostaglandin-driven inflammation. Iron loss begins. Variable performance impact.

**Estrogen** — Estrogen has protective effects on cardiovascular health, bone density, insulin sensitivity, and cognitive function. Its decline in perimenopause and menopause removes these protections, elevating risks across multiple domains simultaneously.

**Progesterone** — Thermogenic, mildly catabolic. Elevates resting metabolic rate slightly in the luteal phase. Disrupts sleep architecture in some women during the luteal phase. Progesterone-dominant cycles (longer luteal phases) may feel harder in training.

**Ferritin and iron** — Menstrual blood loss makes iron depletion a structural risk for female athletes. Ferritin below 30 ng/mL impairs erythropoiesis and aerobic adaptation even before anemia develops. The most commonly missed performance-limiting variable in female athletes. Optimal ferritin for athletic performance is typically cited at >50 ng/mL.

**RED-S (Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport)** — Under-fueling relative to training energy expenditure suppresses the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis, causing hypothalamic amenorrhea. Downstream effects include: cycle loss, stress fractures (low bone density), impaired adaptation, hormonal dysregulation, and increased injury risk. The absence of a menstrual period in a female athlete is not a neutral finding.

**Perimenopause transition** — FSH rise, irregular estradiol fluctuation, progesterone decline. Begins on average in the mid-40s (range 35–55). Sleep disruption, vasomotor symptoms, body composition changes (visceral fat accumulation), mood instability, and cognitive symptoms are common. Standard age-related benchmarks for metabolic and cardiovascular markers shift during this period.

**Bone density** — Estrogen maintains bone density. Post-menopause bone loss accelerates significantly. DEXA bone density should be assessed. Resistance training, calcium, vitamin D, and (where clinically indicated) hormone therapy are the primary interventions.

## Evidence Standards

This bundle favors:
- Female-subject or female-stratified research (and explicitly flags when evidence is from male-dominant studies)
- RCTs and controlled studies of cycle-phase training interventions
- Longitudinal studies of hormonal health and aging in women
- IOC and specialist society consensus statements on female athlete health

This bundle treats with caution:
- Studies that do not account for cycle phase in female participants (a common and significant methodological flaw)
- Hormone therapy claims based on the original Women's Health Initiative (2002) without reference to subsequent reanalysis and updated guidelines
- Supplement studies in female athletes with industry funding

## Known Limits

**Research base is still catching up.** The female athlete research literature is significantly smaller than the male-subject literature. Many findings are based on smaller studies, shorter durations, and less-diverse populations. Hold claims proportionally to evidence strength and be explicit about gaps.

**Hormonal contraception alters the framework.** Combined oral contraceptives suppress endogenous estrogen and progesterone cycling. The phase-based model does not apply in the same way to OCP users. Progestin-only methods and hormonal IUDs each have distinct systemic effects.

**Perimenopause onset and trajectory vary widely.** The 4–10 year transition range means some women experience significant symptoms in their late 30s while others have minimal disruption until the late 40s. Timing and symptom severity are highly individual.

**Hormone therapy risk-benefit has evolved.** The original WHI study overstated risk for HRT/MHT in younger peri/post-menopausal women. Current guidance (Menopause Society, British Menopause Society) supports HRT consideration for appropriate candidates, particularly in early menopause. Route clinical decisions to a specialist rather than applying historical risk framing uncritically.
