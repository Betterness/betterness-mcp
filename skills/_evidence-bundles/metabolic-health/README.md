# Evidence Bundle: Metabolic Health

## Thesis

Metabolic health is the foundation of disease prevention, body composition, cognitive function, and longevity. The majority of chronic diseases — type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, polycystic ovarian syndrome, certain cancers, and Alzheimer's disease — share insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction as upstream drivers. Metabolic health is not binary (diabetic or not) but exists on a continuous spectrum detectable years before clinical disease. The window for effective lifestyle intervention is wide, the tools are available, and the evidence for lifestyle-mediated metabolic reversal is among the strongest in medicine. Glucose stability, insulin sensitivity, and body composition are the three core pillars — each measurable, each responsive to behavioral intervention.

## Key Lenses

**Fasting insulin** — More sensitive early marker for insulin resistance than fasting glucose. Elevated fasting insulin (>10 µIU/mL for adults, >7 µIU/mL as optimal) indicates the pancreas is working harder than normal to maintain euglycemia. This precedes any change in fasting glucose or HbA1c.

**HOMA-IR** — Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance. Calculated from fasting insulin and fasting glucose. HOMA-IR >2.0 indicates insulin resistance in most reference frameworks; >2.5 is clinically significant. A simple, accessible marker that most standard labs can produce.

**Time-in-range (TIR)** — For CGM users, the percentage of time glucose remains within target range (typically 70–140 mg/dL for non-diabetic adults). TIR >80% is associated with better metabolic outcomes. More actionable than HbA1c for real-time feedback.

**Glucose variability (CV%)** — Coefficient of variation of glucose readings. High variability (>36%) even within a normal average range is associated with oxidative stress and may predict worse metabolic outcomes than stable elevated glucose. The pattern of glucose movement matters, not just the mean.

**Triglyceride:HDL ratio** — A practical proxy for insulin resistance when detailed metabolic labs are not available. Ratio >2.0 (using mg/dL) suggests metabolic syndrome risk; >3.0 is significant. A simple, widely available metric with meaningful predictive value.

**Visceral fat** — Metabolically active fat in the abdominal cavity is a direct driver of systemic inflammation and insulin resistance. Waist circumference (>88 cm women, >102 cm men per ATP III criteria) is a clinical proxy. DEXA body composition provides more precise visceral fat area measurement.

**Post-meal glucose response** — The magnitude and duration of glucose elevation after meals is a direct readout of insulin secretion capacity, insulin sensitivity, carbohydrate load, and fiber/fat content of the meal. CGM makes this visible. Target peak postprandial glucose <140 mg/dL and return to baseline within 2 hours.

## Evidence Standards

This bundle favors:
- RCTs of lifestyle interventions (diet, exercise, sleep) with metabolic biomarker outcomes
- Large prospective cohort studies linking metabolic markers to clinical disease incidence
- CGM-based studies in non-diabetic populations (a growing and high-quality literature)
- Mechanistic studies on insulin signaling, hepatic glucose production, and skeletal muscle glucose uptake

This bundle treats with caution:
- Short-duration dietary interventions with surrogate endpoints
- Studies confounded by weight loss (difficult to isolate metabolic effects of specific dietary patterns from caloric deficit effects)
- Industry-sponsored supplement studies with glycemic endpoints

## Known Limits

**HbA1c has significant limitations.** HbA1c reflects average glucose over approximately 3 months but is confounded by hemoglobin variants (sickle cell trait, thalassemia), anemia, and high red blood cell turnover. It also lags the actual metabolic picture. CGM and fasting insulin are more actionable for real-time assessment.

**Individual glucose response variability.** The Weizmann Institute CGM research demonstrated that individuals vary substantially in their glucose response to identical foods — making population-level dietary glycemic index recommendations a rough guide rather than a precise prescription. Personalized CGM data is more useful than glycemic index tables for individual decision-making.

**Medication effects.** Metformin, GLP-1 agonists, corticosteroids, and antipsychotics significantly alter glucose and insulin dynamics. Interpretation of metabolic markers must account for medication context.

**Metabolic health research is disproportionately Western.** Prevalence data, diagnostic thresholds, and intervention studies skew toward Western, high-income populations. Individual and ancestral context should be considered, particularly for waist circumference thresholds, which have different cut-offs for Asian populations in many guidelines.
